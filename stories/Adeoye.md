# From Data Science to Kubernetes: Adeoye's Journey Into DevOps and MLOps

> **CoderLegion Developer Story**

Adeoye's journey into technology has taken him from Geology and Data Science to Machine Learning, cloud computing, DevOps, MLOps, and Kubernetes.

What started with curiosity about computers eventually developed into a deeper interest in understanding what happens underneath modern applications — how systems are deployed, connected, scaled, monitored, and recovered.

In this Developer Story, Adeoye shares his journey, his hands-on #40DaysOfKubernetes learning experience, lessons from troubleshooting Kubernetes systems, and advice for developers moving into DevOps or MLOps.

---

## From Data Science to DevOps

Adeoye's transition into DevOps was not planned as a straight career path.

His early interest in computers eventually led him through cybersecurity, Linux, Geology, data analysis, Data Science, and Machine Learning.

While building and deploying projects, he became increasingly curious about cloud infrastructure and what happens after an application or model has been built.

That curiosity eventually led him to cloud computing, DevOps, containers, and Kubernetes.

Kubernetes became particularly interesting because it helped him understand the infrastructure behind applications rather than focusing only on the application itself.

---

## Why MLOps?

During his Data Science journey, Adeoye experimented with an AI-powered seismic analysis project.

The experience made him realize that building and training a machine-learning model is only part of the problem.

Moving an ML experiment into a reliable real-world system introduces additional challenges:

- Data management
- Compute requirements
- Reproducibility
- Deployment
- Scaling
- Monitoring
- Reliability
- Model performance over time

This is what attracted him to MLOps — understanding everything around the model and how an ML project becomes a useful production system.

---

## Learning Kubernetes by Doing

Adeoye documented his learning through his **#40DaysOfKubernetes** journey.

Writing about what he learns helps him retain technical concepts and identify gaps in his understanding.

One of the biggest lessons from the journey has been troubleshooting.

Instead of simply memorising Kubernetes commands, he learned by encountering problems, investigating them, fixing them, and understanding why the solution worked.

---

## When Kubernetes Breaks

One Kubernetes troubleshooting experience taught Adeoye an important lesson about distributed systems.

When something fails, looking at one application component isn't always enough.

He learned to follow the path of a request through the different layers of the system and investigate how components such as:

- Services
- Endpoints
- Pods
- Network Policies

interact with each other.

Small configuration mismatches can cause communication failures even when the individual Pods appear to be running correctly.

---

## Understanding Instead of Memorising

Adeoye's approach to learning Kubernetes is based on understanding concepts rather than memorising commands.

When he encounters something he doesn't understand, he researches it through documentation, articles, and educational videos.

The goal isn't simply to remember which command fixes a problem.

The goal is to understand **why** the solution works.

That understanding becomes useful when encountering a similar problem in a different environment.

---

## The Hard Parts of Kubernetes

Among the Kubernetes concepts he has worked through, Service Accounts and authentication were particularly challenging.

Understanding the relationship between:

- Service Accounts
- Authentication
- Certificates
- Tokens
- Authorization
- RBAC

helped him distinguish between proving who you are and determining what you are allowed to do.

---

## Why Backups and Recovery Matter

Adeoye's work with etcd backup and recovery reinforced another important lesson:

> Systems should be designed with the expectation that something will eventually go wrong.

Building a system that works is only part of the job.

Developers also need to think about what happens when components fail and whether the system can recover.

---

## Learning in Public

Writing publicly has become an important part of Adeoye's learning process.

Explaining technical concepts forces him to examine his own understanding and can expose gaps that might otherwise remain unnoticed.

His approach is to break complex technical ideas down into simple explanations — similar to explaining something to a complete beginner.

Learning and teaching therefore become part of the same process.

---

## The Developer He's Becoming

Adeoye's mindset has changed from simply making an application work to understanding why the system works.

Instead of immediately looking for a quick fix when something breaks, he wants to understand the underlying components and trace the problem back to its source.

That shift from **"make it work"** to **"understand why it works"** has become an important part of his development as an engineer.

---

## Advice for Developers Entering DevOps or MLOps

For developers, data scientists, and machine-learning practitioners interested in moving into DevOps or MLOps, Adeoye recommends starting with the fundamentals:

1. **Linux**
2. **Networking**
3. **Docker**

These provide a foundation for understanding what happens underneath modern applications before moving into technologies such as Kubernetes.

His advice is also simple:

**Don't focus too heavily on memorising commands.**

Learn how to troubleshoot, understand systems, and figure out why something works or breaks.

Those skills transfer across tools and technologies.

---

## Key Takeaways

Adeoye's journey highlights several lessons:

- Technology careers don't always follow a straight path.
- Data Science can lead naturally into Machine Learning, software development, cloud, DevOps, and MLOps.
- Building a model is different from operating a reliable ML system.
- Kubernetes is easier to understand when you learn by doing and troubleshooting.
- Understanding concepts is more valuable than memorising commands.
- Learning in public can reveal gaps in your own knowledge.
- Developers should think about recovery and failure, not only successful deployments.
- Linux, networking, and Docker provide a strong foundation for learning DevOps.
